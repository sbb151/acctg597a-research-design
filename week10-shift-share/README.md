# Week 10: A Shift-Share Measure of Regulatory Scope

**Paper:** Breuer, M., C. Leuz, and S. Vanhaverbeke (2025), "Reporting Regulation and Corporate Innovation," *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 80(1): 101769.

## The design problem

Every EU limited-liability firm must publish full financial statements unless its country exempts it as small, under thresholds on total assets, sales, and employees that differ across countries and that the authors call persistent over time (pp. 2, 6; fn. 15). The scope of the mandate in a country-industry is the share of its firms above the thresholds, and that share is endogenous: "technology shocks causing firms in certain industries to grow above the thresholds" and "omitted factors correlated with firm sizes in certain countries (e.g., industrial policies)" both move it (p. 2). Breuer, Leuz, and Vanhaverbeke therefore replace the actual share with a simulated one: "the hypothetical share of firms that would exceed a given country's exemption thresholds if its thresholds were applied to a Europe-wide firm-size distribution," pooled across all countries and years (p. 9). In shift-share terms the industry size distributions are the shares (exposure) and the countries' thresholds are the shifts; the authors call the measure "a variant of the Bartik instrument" (p. 2) and use it directly as the regressor, with country-year and industry-year fixed effects and two-way clustering, so that the identifying variation is the interaction of a country's threshold level with an industry's size structure. "This design does not rely on changes in countries' thresholds over time" (p. 2). They find that a 10 percentage point rise in simulated scope lowers the share of innovating firms by 1.8 points, about 5 percent of the mean (Table 2, p. 12).

The design removes the firms' own size responses by construction, which is its strength. What it cannot remove is a factor correlated with the exposure-by-threshold interaction itself: the identifying assumption is that "there are no omitted factors correlated with corporate innovation and our intensity measure at the country-industry level" (p. 9). The shift-share literature has learned to ask which route to that claim a paper takes, exogenous shares (Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift 2020) or exogenous shifts (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2022), which units carry the estimate (Rotemberg weights), whether the coefficient is a reduced form (the paper reports no first stage of the actual share on the simulated share), and whether standard errors reflect correlation across units with similar shares (Adão, Kolesár, and Morales 2019). The module makes each point concrete with a known truth.

## The simulation module

`shift_share_simulation.do` (base Stata with Mata, about six minutes, seed 2025) builds thirty industries, ten countries, eight years, and fifty firms per cell. Base log size is normal with an industry-specific mean; six size bins are cut at the five threshold values; each country's threshold takes one of those values, and three countries move theirs once. The simulated scope is the share of the industry's base-period firms above the country's threshold, the actual share is computed from current sizes, which respond to innovation shocks with strength λ, and the outcome's causal response to the mandate's scope is −0.50 by construction. Two fixed-effect structures are exact demeanings on the balanced grid: industry-year and country-year effects (the paper's) and the same plus country-industry effects (the three-factor-interaction residual, which keeps only the threshold changes).

1. **One draw and the identities.** OLS on the actual share is about +2.8 against a causal effect of −0.50; the reduced form on the simulated scope is about −0.48 and 2SLS about −0.60 (standard error 0.24, clustered by country-year) under the paper's effects. The script asserts that `regress` and `ivregress` reproduce the demeaned ratios and the Mata clustered standard error, that 2SLS equals the reduced form over the first stage, that the Rotemberg weights by size bin and the country weights sum to one and the weighted just-identified estimates sum to 2SLS, and that with country-industry effects added a scope built from the initial thresholds alone demeans to zero. Threshold levels explain about 83 percent of the variance of the demeaned measure under the paper's effects.
2. **Reverse causality (grid a).** As λ rises from 0 to 0.5, mean OLS moves from −0.49 to +2.77 while the reduced form stays near −0.44 and 2SLS near −0.50 (first-stage F from about 1,300 to 470). Output: `figures/fig1_reverse_causality.png`.
3. **An omitted country-industry factor (grid b).** The outcome also contains γ times the share of the industry's firms in the size bin just above the country's initial threshold, the incidence of a bundled size-based rule (an audit exemption, a simplified format) that shares the thresholds but is not the mandate; its correlation with the demeaned simulated scope is about 0.15 in one draw. Mean 2SLS under the paper's effects moves from −0.46 at γ = 0 to −0.38 at γ = 1 and −0.25 at γ = 2 (four times the causal effect) while F stays near 460; with country-industry effects added, the estimate stays near −0.60 with a standard deviation across draws of about 1.1 and F near 50. Output: `figures/fig2_exclusion_violation.png`.
4. **Few countries (grid c).** With a country-level factor whose incidence scales with an industry's share of small firms (SD 0.3), the standard deviation of 2SLS across draws falls from 1.09 with 3 countries to 0.32 with 20, and the median largest country weight (the country analogue of a Rotemberg weight) from 1.57 to 0.28. The mean standard error two-way clustered by country-industry and country-year (the paper's scheme) is 0.62 and 0.22, rejecting the true effect at the 5 percent level in 25 and 18 percent of draws; clustered by country, the level of the shock, it is 0.61 and 0.31, rejecting in 34 percent (three clusters) and 6 percent (twenty). Output: `figures/fig3_few_countries.png`.
5. **Compliance (grid d).** When each firm above the threshold complies with probability e, the first stage falls from 0.85 at e = 1 to 0.17 at e = 0.2, the reduced form from −0.41 to −0.07, and 2SLS stays near −0.50 (−0.44 at e = 0.2, F = 82). Output: `figures/fig4_compliance.png`.

Monte Carlo means over 300 draws per point; Stata's draws reproduce these numbers exactly with the stated seed. The deck's data file `output/sim-bartik.js` is written as a by-product.

## Key readings

- Breuer, Leuz, and Vanhaverbeke (2025, *JAE*): the focal paper; the simulated-instrument intensity (pp. 8-9), the identifying assumption and its institutional defence (p. 9), the German enforcement reform as the second design (pp. 9, 15-17).
- Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift (2020, *AER*): the shares as instruments, Rotemberg weights, and the diagnostics of the exposure design.
- Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022, *REStud*; 2025, *JEP*): the shocks as instruments, the many-shocks requirement, and the shock-level regression; Borusyak and Hull (2023, *Econometrica*) on recentering formula-based and simulated instruments.
- Adão, Kolesár, and Morales (2019, *QJE*): inference when residuals are correlated across units with similar shares.
- Breuer (2022, *Journal of Financial Reporting*): an applied introduction to Bartik instruments for accounting researchers; Breuer (2021, *JAR*) and its 2025 correction: the construction of the standardized scopes in the same setting.
- Currie and Gruber (1996, *JPE*): the simulated-instrument idea the measure follows.
- Bernard, Burgstahler, and Kaya (2018, *JAE*): size management below EU reporting thresholds.
