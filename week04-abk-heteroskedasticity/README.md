# Week 4: Heteroskedasticity of Known and Unknown Form

**Paper:** Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik (1999), "Revaluations of Fixed Assets and Future Firm Performance: Evidence from the UK," *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 26(1-3): 149-178.

## The design problem

Equation (1) of Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik (ABK) regresses the one- to three-year-ahead change in operating income on the current-year fixed-asset revaluation, with year intercepts, after deflating both variables by beginning-of-year market value of equity (p. 160). The paper then reports that "Reported t-statistics are based on White (1980) standard errors (Barth and Kallapur, 1996), and the estimation controls for outliers using a robust regression technique" (p. 162). The paper therefore corrects for heteroskedasticity twice, by deflation and by White's estimator, and adds a residual-based reweighting (fn 17) on top. The module asks what each correction assumes and when each is the appropriate one.

The general principle is an identity. With Var(u_i | x) = sigma_i^2, the OLS slope remains unbiased, but its true sampling variance is sum(x~_i^2 sigma_i^2)/(sum x~_i^2)^2, where x~ is the regressor after partialling out the other regressors, whereas the conventional formula targets sigma-bar^2/sum x~_i^2; the ratio is 1 + Cov(x~^2, sigma^2)/(mean x~^2 times mean sigma^2). Heteroskedasticity misstates the standard error only through the covariance between the squared regressor and the error variance. White (1980) replaces sigma_i^2 with the squared residual and is consistent under any form; deflating every variable by a scale variable z is weighted least squares with weight 1/z^2 and is the efficient, correctly sized estimator only when the error standard deviation is exactly proportional to z. When the exponent is wrong, the deflated regression is still heteroskedastic, its conventional t-test is mis-sized in the direction the covariance term gives, and the deflated slope can be far less precise than the levels slope. A deflator is therefore a bet on the form of the variance, and a heteroskedasticity-consistent standard error is insurance against the bet being wrong; ABK's practice of doing both is the sensible one, with the caveat that the HC1 version of White's estimator is biased downward in levels regressions with a skewed scale variable at samples of a few hundred firms, where HC3 is advisable.

## The simulation modules

`abk_heteroskedasticity.do` (base Stata, about three minutes) runs three self-contained modules with seed 1999. The data-generating process is calibrated to ABK Table 2: z is beginning market value of equity (log z ~ N(4, 1)); the revaluation ratio r = REV/MVE is gamma-distributed with mean about 0.11 and standard deviation about 0.18, declining in size with elasticity -0.25; x = REV = z r; the change in operating income is y = 0.02 z + 0.07 x + u with error standard deviation 0.065 z^(gamma/2), scaled so that it equals 0.065 z at the median firm for every gamma; N = 740. The true slope is 0.07 (ABK Table 3, one-year horizon). Identity checks are printed in the log and asserted.

1. **Identities in one sample (gamma = 2).** The levels regression y on x, z, and a constant; the deflated regression y/z on x/z, 1/z, and a constant. Checks: Stata's `vce(robust)` equals the hand-computed sandwich times n/(n - k) to eight decimals; the deflated OLS slope, standard error, and intercept equal `regress y x z [aweight = 1/z^2]`; the ratio of the true slope variance (with sigma_i^2 known) to the conventional target equals 1 + Cov(x~^2, sigma^2)/(mean x~^2 times mean sigma^2), about 6.2 in this sample. The table reports five standard errors for the levels slope (conventional 0.022, HC1 0.031, HC3 0.033, true 0.055) and two for the deflated slope (0.013 under either formula). Output: `tables/tab_abk_heteroskedasticity_identity.tex`.
2. **Residual fans.** The same draws plotted three ways: levels residuals against log MVE (a fan widening with size), deflated residuals at gamma = 2 (constant spread; size-tercile standard-deviation ratio 1.0), and deflated residuals when the error standard deviation is proportional to MVE^(1/2) (the band narrows with size; ratio 0.3). Output: `figures/fig1_abk_heteroskedasticity_fan.png`.
3. **Monte Carlo over the form.** Ten designs, 1,000 samples of N = 740 each: error standard deviation proportional to z^(gamma/2) for gamma = 0, 0.5, ..., 4, plus a regressor-dependent variance (three times the standard deviation for above-median revaluation ratios) that no scale deflator removes. Rejection rates of the true null at 5 percent: levels conventional 0.05 at gamma = 0 rising to 0.60 at gamma = 4; deflated conventional 0.19 at gamma = 0, 0.05 at gamma = 2, 0.003 at gamma = 4; levels HC1 0.07 to 0.14 and HC3 0.03 to 0.07; deflated HC1 0.05 to 0.07 in every design, including the regressor-dependent one (0.07 against 0.12 conventional). The sampling standard deviation of the deflated slope relative to the levels slope is 6.5 at gamma = 0, 1.07 at gamma = 1, 0.21 at gamma = 2, and 0.017 at gamma = 4. Outputs: `tables/tab_abk_heteroskedasticity_montecarlo.tex`, `figures/fig2_abk_heteroskedasticity_size.png`, `figures/fig3_abk_heteroskedasticity_efficiency.png`.

## Key readings

- Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik (1999, *Journal of Accounting and Economics*): the focal paper; deflates equation (1) by beginning market value of equity, reports White (1980) t-statistics, and reweights observations on absolute residuals (fn 17), finding a positive relation between current revaluations and future operating income at every horizon (Table 3).
- White (1980, *Econometrica*): the heteroskedasticity-consistent covariance matrix estimator, consistent for the sampling variance of OLS under heteroskedasticity of unknown form, together with a direct test for heteroskedasticity.
- Barth and Kallapur (1996, *Contemporary Accounting Research*): cited by ABK as the authority for both corrections; studies how cross-sectional scale differences bias coefficients and distort inference in levels regressions, and compares deflation, a scale proxy as a regressor, and heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors as remedies.
- MacKinnon and White (1985, *Journal of Econometrics*): introduces the HC1 to HC3 variants of White's estimator and finds the jackknife-type HC3 performs best in small samples; Long and Ervin (2000, *The American Statistician*): recommends HC3 in samples below about 250 observations because HC0 and HC1 are biased downward at high-leverage points.
