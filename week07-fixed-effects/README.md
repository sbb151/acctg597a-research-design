# Week 7: Fixed Effects, the Variation They Leave and the Comparison They Draw

**Papers:** Mummolo, J., and E. Peterson (2018), "Improving the Interpretation of Fixed Effects Regression Results," *Political Science Research and Methods* 6(4): 829-835. Kropko, J., and R. Kubinec (2020), "Interpretation and Identification of Within-Unit and Cross-Sectional Variation in Panel Data Models," *PLOS ONE* 15(4): e0231349. Running example: Anantharaman, D., and Y. G. Lee (2014), "Managerial Risk Taking Incentives and Corporate Pension Policy," *Journal of Financial Economics* 111(2): 328-351.

## The design problem

A fixed effects coefficient is estimated only from the variation the fixed effects leave. With firm effects, that is each firm's deviation from its own mean; with firm and year effects, it is the part of the regressor that is neither a firm component nor a year component. Mummolo and Peterson show that applied work routinely describes such coefficients with the pooled standard deviation of the regressor, although "social scientists often fail to acknowledge the large reduction in variation imposed by fixed effects" (p. 829); in their five replications the identifying variation is 24 to 82 percent of the pooled standard deviation. Anantharaman and Lee (2014) are a typical accounting case: they standardize CFO vega by its pooled standard deviation (p. 334) and keep that scale in their firm fixed effects tables, and they report no within-firm statistics.

Kropko and Kubinec ask the prior question. A one-way fixed effects slope is a variance-weighted average of subset slopes: firm effects average each firm's time-series slope (an over-time comparison), and year effects average each year's cross-sectional slope (a cross-sectional comparison). The two can differ, as Anantharaman and Lee's industry-and-year (Table 5) and firm-and-year (Table 6A) estimates do. Two-way effects draw a third comparison and are identified only by the part of the regressor that is not additive in firm and year; when that part is small the estimate is imprecise, and when it is absent the slope is not identified. The fixed effects should follow from the comparison the hypothesis states, and the magnitude should be stated in the variation that remains.

## The simulation modules

`fixed_effects.do` (base Stata, under a minute) runs four self-contained modules on one simulated panel of standardized CFO vega: 900 firms over 5 years, seed 2014, with 25 percent of vega's sum of squares within firms (an assumption, because Anantharaman and Lee report no within statistics) and 72 firms whose vega never changes. Identity checks are printed in the log and should be zero to ten decimals.

1. **The variation a fixed effects coefficient uses (Mummolo and Peterson).** Underfunding responds to vega with slope 0.0176 (Anantharaman and Lee's Table 4A, Model 3, evaluated at their 95th percentile of distress), plus a firm trait correlated with vega. Firm-and-year dummies, `xtreg, fe`, and the regression on vega residualized on the fixed effects (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell) give one slope, 0.0158 (t = 2.47); the total sum of squares equals within plus between; the firm-effects slope is a weighted average of firm slopes with weight zero for constant-vega firms (with year effects those firms keep 1.2 percent of the two-way sum of squares, as comparisons). The standard deviation of vega is 1.00 pooled, 0.50 after firm effects, and 0.47 after firm and year effects, so an effect stated per pooled SD is about twice the effect per within SD. Output: `figures/fig1_within_variation.png`.
2. **Which comparison each choice draws (Kropko and Kubinec).** Vega splits exactly into a between-firm part (75.0 percent of its sum of squares), a year part (3.3 percent), and a residual part (21.7 percent), each with its own slope in the pension equity share (18.3, 30, and 2.6 per 1,000, chosen so that the year-effects and two-way slopes match Anantharaman and Lee's Tables 5 and 6A at the 95th percentile of distress). On the systematic part, pooled, year, firm, and two-way slopes are 15.3, 14.8, 6.2, and 2.6, each the share-weighted average of the slopes on the parts it keeps. The log also checks Kropko and Kubinec's Eqs. 8, 10-11, 12, and 13, and the gap identity: firm effects minus two-way effects equals the estimated year effects times the year means of vega, over the within sum of squares. Output: `tables/tab_fe_slices.tex`.
3. **Identification.** A Monte Carlo (500 draws per point, in Mata) shrinks the residual part of vega from about 25 percent of its variance to 0.002 percent; the sampling SD of the two-way slope rises from 2.9 to 309 per 1,000, matching the analytical value to within 2 percent. At zero the two-way slope is not identified. Output: `figures/fig2_twoway_precision.png`.
4. **Kropko and Kubinec's homogeneous design.** With homogeneous within-year (1) and within-firm (-3) slopes, their data-generating process makes x exactly additive in firm and year (Eq. 21); Stata drops one more dummy and reports -3.000 or 1.000 depending only on the order in which the dummies are listed (K&K p. 18).

## Key readings

- Mummolo and Peterson (2018, *PSRM*): the reporting checklist (residualize the regressor on the fixed effects, state effects per within SD, report the share of units without within variation, describe the variation in within terms).
- Kropko and Kubinec (2020, *PLOS ONE*): one-way fixed effects as weighted averages of subset slopes; the two-way comparison; the unidentification result in their design, which holds when the regressor has no firm-year variation of its own.
- Anantharaman and Lee (2014, *JFE*): the running example; industry-and-year ("Cross-sectional tests," Tables 3 and 5) and firm-and-year (Tables 4 and 6) estimates of the effect of CFO vega on pension risk shifting.
- Plumper and Troeger (2007, *Political Analysis*), as cited by Mummolo and Peterson: units without within variation provide no information to one-way unit fixed effects estimation.
