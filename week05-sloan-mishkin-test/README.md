# Week 5: The Mishkin Test as a Return Regression

**Paper:** Sloan (1996), "Do Stock Prices Fully Reflect Information in Accruals and Cash Flows about Future Earnings?" *The Accounting Review* 71(3): 289-315.

## The design problem

Sloan tests whether stock prices reflect the lower persistence of accruals with the framework of Mishkin (1983). A forecasting equation, Earnings(t+1) = g0 + g1 Accruals(t) + g2 CashFlows(t) + v (Sloan eq. 10), is estimated jointly with a pricing equation, AbnRet(t+1) = b (Earnings(t+1) - g0 - g1* Accruals(t) - g2* CashFlows(t)) + e (Sloan eq. 11), by iterative weighted nonlinear least squares. Market efficiency imposes g1 = g1* and g2 = g2*, and the restriction is tested with the likelihood ratio statistic 2n log(SSRc/SSRu) (Sloan eq. 12). Sloan reports g1 = 0.765 against g1* = 0.911 and an LR statistic of 180.91 (Table 5, p. 305), and he notes that the tests "remain valid tests of market efficiency regardless of whether the forecasting equation has omitted variables" (fn. 13, p. 302).

The general principle is an identity. Substituting the forecasting equation into the pricing equation gives AbnRet(t+1) = b v + b (g1 - g1*) Accruals(t) + b (g2 - g2*) CashFlows(t) + e, so the Mishkin restriction holds if and only if accruals and cash flows do not predict abnormal returns. Three consequences follow. First, the unconstrained Mishkin estimates are an exact reparameterization of OLS (g_k* = -d_k / b, where b and d_k are the OLS slopes of abnormal returns on future earnings and on component k), and the LR statistic is asymptotically the OLS Wald statistic (Abel and Mishkin 1983; Kraft, Leone, and Wasley 2007). Second, the robustness to omitted variables applies to the joint test of efficiency, not to the attribution of mispricing to a particular variable: an omitted variable that is correlated with accruals and is itself mispriced shifts g1* (Kraft, Leone, and Wasley 2007, eq. 11). Third, the implied misperception g1* - g1 equals minus the return slope divided by the earnings response coefficient, so its magnitude depends on the pricing model that the researcher assumes (Lewellen 2010, pp. 460-461). Pooled LR statistics also inherit the cross-sectional correlation problem of pooled return regressions.

## The simulation modules

`sloan-mishkin-test.do` (base Stata, several minutes because Modules 2 and 4 are Monte Carlo experiments) runs six self-contained modules with seed 1996. The program `mishkin`, defined at the top of the file, implements the test by stacking the two equations and weighting each by the inverse of its residual variance. The common data-generating process uses Sloan's Table 5 values: Accruals ~ N(0, 0.08^2); CashFlows = -0.5 Accruals + N(0, 0.08^2); forecasting weights 0.765 and 0.855; earnings response coefficient 1.894; forecasting and pricing error standard deviations 0.06 and 0.30. Identity checks are printed in the log and asserted.

1. **The unconstrained Mishkin estimates are OLS.** N = 40,679; the market's weights are 0.911 and 0.826. The nonlinear system returns g1* = 0.919 and g2* = 0.832, identical to -d_k / b from OLS to six decimals; LR = 245.2 against an OLS Wald statistic of 245.7. Output: `tables/tab_sloan_mishkin_test_identity.tex`.
2. **The LR statistic tracks the OLS Wald statistic.** 300 samples of N = 2,000 with the market's accrual weight varied across samples. The correlation between the two statistics is 0.99999, the mean relative difference is 0.2 percent, and the two tests disagree at the 5 percent level in 1 of 300 samples. Output: `figures/fig2_sloan_mishkin_test_lrwald.png`.
3. **An omitted, mispriced correlate.** Accruals and cash flows are rationally priced. A third variable Z = 0.5 Accruals + u does not forecast earnings, but the market prices it as if its weight were 0.292, so the analytical gap is 0.292 x 0.5 = 0.146 (Sloan's Table 5 gap, by construction). With Z omitted, the test reports g1* = 0.916 against g1 = 0.762 and LR = 215.0; with Z included, g1* = 0.776 and the mispricing is assigned to Z. Output: `tables/tab_sloan_mishkin_test_omitted.tex`.
4. **Pooled LR statistics and cross-sectional correlation.** The null is true: 30 years of 500 firms, with a yearly return loading on accruals drawn from N(0, 0.40^2). Across 500 replications, the pooled Mishkin LR test and the pooled OLS Wald test each reject in 35.6 percent of samples at the nominal 5 percent level, whereas the Fama-MacBeth t-test over 30 annual slopes rejects in 5.8 percent. Output: `tables/tab_sloan_mishkin_test_size.tex`.
5. **Sloan's Tables 4 and 5 recreated.** Forecasting coefficients against the coefficients implied by prices, with the 0.841 fixation benchmark. Output: `figures/fig1_sloan_mishkin_test_table5.png`.
6. **The implied bias varies inversely with the earnings response coefficient.** The curve g1* - g1 = -(return slope)/ERC for the return slopes implied by Sloan (1996) and by Dechow, Richardson, and Sloan (2008), recreated from Lewellen (2010, p. 461). Output: `figures/fig3_sloan_mishkin_test_erc.png`.

## Key readings

- Sloan (1996, *The Accounting Review*): the focal paper; finds that accruals are less persistent than cash flows, that prices appear to overweight accrual persistence (Mishkin test, Table 5), and that a hedge portfolio on accruals earns 10.4 percent in the following year (Table 6).
- Mishkin (1983, *A Rational Expectations Approach to Macroeconometrics*, University of Chicago Press): the origin of the test; joint nonlinear estimation of forecasting and pricing equations with cross-equation restrictions and the likelihood ratio statistic 2n log(SSRc/SSRu).
- Abel and Mishkin (1983, *Journal of Monetary Economics*): show that the cross-equation test is asymptotically equivalent to regressing the deviation of the return from its equilibrium value on past information.
- Kraft, Leone, and Wasley (2007, *Journal of Accounting Research*): show that the Mishkin test and OLS yield virtually identical estimates and t-statistics in large accounting samples, that omitted variables are irrelevant only if they are rationally priced, and that the accrual t-statistic falls from -5.41 in the pooled test to -1.68 in year-by-year estimation (Table 3).
- Lewellen (2010, *Journal of Accounting and Economics*): argues that the Mishkin test is conceptually identical to a return predictability test and that it assumes, rather than provides evidence, that predictability arises from misperception of the earnings process.
