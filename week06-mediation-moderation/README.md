# Week 6: Direct, Indirect, and Conditional Effects

**Papers:** Bhattacharya, Ecker, Olsson, and Schipper (2012), "Direct and Mediated Associations among Earnings Quality, Information Asymmetry, and the Cost of Equity," *The Accounting Review* 87(2): 449-482 (the focal application); Hayes and Rockwood (2017), "Regression-based statistical mediation and moderation analysis in clinical research: Observations, recommendations, and implementation," *Behaviour Research and Therapy* 98: 39-57 (the methods source); Lennox and Payne-Mann (2026), "An Explanation of Path Analysis and Recommendations for Best Practice," *Contemporary Accounting Research* 43(1): 290-313.

## The design problem

Bhattacharya et al. "use path analysis to decompose the correlation between the source (causal) variable earnings quality and the outcome variable cost of equity into direct and indirect (mediated) paths" (p. 452). In their Table 2 (p. 463) the correlation of 0.2264 between accruals quality and the cost of equity splits into a direct path of 0.1827 (80.69 percent) and a mediated path through price impact of 0.1627 x 0.2686 = 0.0437 (19.31 percent). The module asks what such a split estimates, how each piece is tested, and how a mediation question ("how does X affect Y?") differs from a moderation question ("when, or for whom, does X affect Y?").

The general principles, in Hayes and Rockwood's notation, are three. First, when M is regressed on X and Y is regressed on X and M by OLS on one sample with the same covariates in every equation, the total effect decomposes exactly as c = c' + ab; a mediated share ab/c is therefore arithmetic rather than a test, the size of c neither determines nor constrains ab, and inference belongs on the product ab, whose sampling distribution is skewed, so that a percentile bootstrap interval is preferred to the normal-theory (Sobel) test. Second, in Y = i + b1 X + b2 W + b3 XW the effect of X is the conditional effect b1 + b3 W: b3 tests moderation, b1 is the effect at W = 0 rather than a main effect, centering only relabels b1, and the effect is probed by the Johnson-Neyman boundaries computed from the fitted model rather than by sample cutpoints or subgroup splits. When W moderates the a path, the indirect effect is itself a line in W, (a1 + a3 W) b, whose slope a3 b is the index of moderated mediation. Third, the identity fixes the sum but not the split: an unobserved variable in both M and Y, or measurement error in M, moves the total between the direct and indirect paths while leaving the total effect of an exogenous X unbiased (Lennox and Payne-Mann 2026, pp. 292-294), so the causal reading of a split rests on the mediator being unconfounded with the outcome and measured without error.

## The simulation modules

`mediation_moderation.do` (base Stata; about three minutes, almost all of it in Module 2) runs five self-contained modules. Identity checks are printed in the log and asserted; Monte Carlo properties are compared with their population values at a stated tolerance.

1. **The decomposition holds even when c is zero.** N = 5,000 (seed 2012); M = 0.5 X + e_M; Design A has Y = 0.2 X + 0.8 M + e_Y and Design B has Y = -0.4 X + 0.8 M + e_Y, with one draw of X and e_M for both. The log shows c - (c' + ab) = 0.00000000 in each design, and `sem (m <- x) (yA <- x m)` returns the same a, b, and c' as the three OLS regressions. Design B pairs an indirect effect of 0.38 (Sobel z about 30) with a total effect of -0.01 (t = -0.8), so a test of the total effect cannot serve as a gate for the indirect effect (Hayes and Rockwood 2017, p. 43). Output: `tables/tab_mediation_moderation_identity.tex`.
2. **The product ab has a skewed sampling distribution.** 5,000 samples of N = 100 (seed 2017) from M = 0.25 X + e_M, Y = 0.10 X + 0.25 M + e_Y, so ab = 0.0625. In every sample a Sobel interval and a 1,000-resample percentile bootstrap interval (computed in Mata for speed) are formed. Skewness of ab-hat is about 0.78; the Sobel interval covers the true value in about 92 percent of samples and rejects zero in about 25 percent, against about 94 and 44 percent for the percentile bootstrap. The module ends with the recipe on one sample using Stata's `bootstrap` command with a two-regression program. Output: `figures/fig2_mediation_moderation_bootstrap.png`.
3. **The effect of X is a line in W.** N = 500 (seed 2017); Y = 0.15 X + 0.30 W + 0.25 XW + e with X and W standard normal. The conditional effect b1 + b3 W is about 0.15 + 0.31 W; the mean-centered b1 equals b1 + b3 Wbar and b3 is unchanged (checked to 1e-6); the Johnson-Neyman boundaries, the roots of (b1 + b3 W)^2 = t^2 Var(b1 + b3 W), fall near W = -0.89 and -0.21, and the log confirms that the t statistic of the conditional effect equals the critical value at both roots. A median split of W gives slopes of about -0.06 (t = -0.9) and 0.37 (t = 5.9); the contrast is not the test of b3 (t = 6.7). Output: `figures/fig3_mediation_moderation_conditional.png`, `tables/tab_mediation_moderation_probe.tex`.
4. **Test the index of moderated mediation.** N = 500 (seed 2017); M = 0.5 X + 0.2 W + 0.3 XW + e_M and Y = 0.2 X + 0.6 M + e_Y, estimated with W and XW also in the Y equation (PROCESS model 8 of Hayes 2013). The conditional indirect effect (a1 + a3 W) b is about 0.31 + 0.22 W against the true 0.30 + 0.18 W; the index a3 b is about 0.22 with a percentile bootstrap interval of roughly [0.16, 0.28] from 1,000 resamples of (a1, a3, b). The reduced-form regression of Y on X, W, and XW returns c1' + b a1 and c3' + b a3 exactly. Output: `figures/fig4_mediation_moderation_modmed.png`.
5. **The identity fixes the sum, not the split.** 500 samples of N = 2,000 (seed 2012). Design A is clean (true c' = 0.2, ab = 0.4, c = 0.6). Design B adds an unobserved U with loading 0.6 to both M and Y, so b-hat converges to 0.8 + 0.36/1.36 = 1.065 and the split becomes 0.07 direct and 0.53 indirect (89 percent "mediated"). Design C observes M with unit-variance noise, so b-hat converges to 0.4 and the split becomes 0.40 and 0.20 (33 percent). In all 1,500 design-samples c' + ab = c to 1e-6, and the Monte Carlo means are within 0.01 of the population values derived in the deck. Output: `figures/fig5_mediation_moderation_paths.png`.

## Key readings

- Bhattacharya, Ecker, Olsson, and Schipper (2012, *The Accounting Review*): the focal paper; decomposes the earnings-quality and cost-of-equity correlation into a direct path and paths mediated by information asymmetry and by Beta, and finds the direct path dominant (73 to 92 percent without controls); concedes in footnote 26 that measurement error in the mediator understates the mediated share.
- Hayes and Rockwood (2017, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*): the regression-based framework used here; argues for testing the indirect effect ab once with a bootstrap interval rather than through causal steps or the Sobel test, that a significant total effect is not a prerequisite, that b1 and b2 are conditional effects at zero in a model with a product, that centering does not change the test of b3, and that moderated mediation is tested through the index a3 b1.
- Lennox and Payne-Mann (2026, *Contemporary Accounting Research*): shows that path analysis under uncorrelated errors is OLS equation by equation and under correlated errors requires an exclusion restriction (instrumental variables); with an endogenous mediator the direct and indirect estimates are biased while the total effect and the X-to-M path are not, even when X is manipulated; surveys 193 accounting studies and recommends disclosing and justifying the error assumption.
- Baron and Kenny (1986, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*): the causal-steps procedure that the modern framework replaces; introduced the a, b, c, c' labels and the three-regression implementation.
- Callen, Livnat, and Segal (2009, *The Accounting Review*), the Week 3 module: controlling for the credit rating, a mediator between earnings and CDS premia, returns the direct rather than the total effect; the shared-shock design of that module reappears here as Design B of Module 5.
